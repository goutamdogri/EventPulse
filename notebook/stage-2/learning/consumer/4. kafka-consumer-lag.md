# Kafka Consumer: Lag

*Consumer note 4 of the Stage 2 learning notes (the Kafka series is in `../Kafka/`, the producer series in `../producer/`). Read Kafka notes 2 (offsets) and 5 (retention), and consumer note 3 (commits) first. Lag is the log end offset minus the committed offset, per partition, and it is the main health signal of a consumer. This note defines it precisely, explains how it is measured (and why every way of measuring it is slightly wrong), gives the arithmetic of lag growth and drain, shows how to read its shapes, and maps it to your `consumer-sink` (lag monitor, metrics, `/debug/stats`). It supports Step 6 (consumer metrics), Step 7 (the audit's "drained" check) and experiments K3, K6/K7 (slow database), K8 and K9.*

**What was run.** **No labs were run for this note.** The numbers marked **[observed]** come from runs recorded in earlier notes on this repo's cluster (for example the `kafka-consumer-groups.sh --describe` output in Kafka note 2, and the per-partition skew of 34.5 % in producer note 6) or from reading this repo's source (`services/consumer-sink/src/observability/lag.ts`, `sink-stats.ts`). The formulas and the pattern table are **[reasoning]**: standard queueing arithmetic and standard consumer behavior. The worked examples use **illustrative** rates, not measurements of your pipeline.

## 1. TL;DR

- **Lag = log end offset − committed offset**, **per partition**; **total lag** is the sum. It answers "how many records are in the log that this group has **not yet finished**".
- It is the **first signal to look at** for a consumer, because it is the one place where **producers and consumers meet**: lag rises when records arrive faster than the group finishes them, and falls when it catches up. The queue's whole promise in Stage 2 is that a slow database shows up as **lag, not as 503s**.
- Because "finished" means **committed**, lag is measured from **committed offsets**, so it is **an upper bound that sawtooths** with the commit interval: records processed but not yet committed still count as lag (consumer note 3, section 7).
- **Records of lag are not time.** 60,000 records is 30 s at 2,000 events/s and 20 minutes at 50 events/s. What matters to users and to **retention** is **time-lag**: how old the oldest unprocessed record is. Keep both (`ep_consumer_lag` and `ep_event_freshness_seconds`).
- The arithmetic is simple: **`lag(t) = lag₀ + (arrival rate − drain rate) × t`**. If the drain rate is below the arrival rate, lag grows without bound; if above, it drains at **`lag / (drain − arrival)`** seconds.
- **Read it per partition.** One partition growing while others sit at zero means a **stuck partition** (poison message, a stuck owner) or a **hot key**; a total hides it. With the measured skew (producer note 6), the busiest partition's lag grows first.
- Zero lag means **committed everything**, **not** "everything is correct in Postgres": it is only equal to "stored" if the commit order is right (consumer note 3). Lag also says nothing about **duplicates** or **poison records**.
- The sink polls lag every `LAG_POLL_MS` (2 s) with a separate admin client, caches it, and serves `/metrics` and `/debug/stats` from the cache; it also reports a cheap **local lag** from the batch it just read.

> **One-line version:** *Lag is the size of the unfinished backlog, per partition; watch its trend and its age, not just its value.*

## 2. The mental model

A water tank with an inlet and an outlet.

| Tank | Consumer |
| --- | --- |
| Water arriving through the inlet | Records appended to the log (the arrival rate) |
| Water leaving through the outlet | Records the group finishes and commits (the drain rate) |
| The water level | **Lag** |
| Inlet larger than outlet: the level climbs until it overflows | Arrival faster than drain: lag grows without bound (until retention deletes the oldest data, Kafka note 5) |
| Outlet larger than inlet: the level falls to the bottom | The group catches up; lag approaches zero |
| A float that only moves when someone **writes down** the reading | Lag is read from **committed** offsets, not from the live position |
| Six separate tanks (partitions) feeding one meter | Total lag hides which tank is full |
| How long the oldest drop has been waiting | **Time-lag** (what retention and users care about) |

## 3. Definition

For each partition `p` of the topic and one consumer group:

```
 lag(p)    =  logEndOffset(p)  −  committedOffset(p)
 totalLag  =  Σ lag(p)
```

- **Log end offset** is the offset the **next** record will get (Kafka note 2, section 3): "last record + 1". The value consumers can actually read up to is the **high watermark**; for fully replicated partitions they coincide, and tools report the end offset as the high watermark.
- **Committed offset** is the offset of the **next record to read** (last processed + 1). Both are "next" numbers, so there is **no off-by-one**: a group that finished everything has `committed = end` and **lag 0** (Kafka note 2, section 3).

Variants you will meet:

| Name | Formula | Where it appears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Group lag** (committed-based) | end − committed | `kafka-consumer-groups.sh`, the admin API, the sink's `ep_consumer_lag` |
| **Consumer (position) lag** | end − position | Client metrics; smaller than group lag because position runs ahead of the commit |
| **Local lag** | high watermark − last offset in the batch just fetched | The sink's `local_lag` in `/debug/stats` (cheap, this member only) |
| **Time-lag** | age of the oldest unprocessed record | `ep_event_freshness_seconds` (age of a just-processed record), or derived |
| **Byte lag** | bytes between committed and end | Rarely used; disk or network view |

**A partition the group never committed** has no committed offset (`-` in the CLI; `-1` in the admin API). The sink counts its lag as **everything still in the log**, `end − log start`, "which is what a member starting from the beginning would have to read" **[observed in `computeLag`]**. Compare Kafka note 2, section 9: with `earliest` a new group really will read all of it; with `latest` it would skip it, so for a `latest` group the number would be an over-report.

## 4. Ways to measure

### 4.1 The CLI

```bash
K kafka-consumer-groups.sh --bootstrap-server $B --describe --group sink-postgres
# GROUP  TOPIC  PARTITION  CURRENT-OFFSET  LOG-END-OFFSET  LAG  CONSUMER-ID  HOST  CLIENT-ID
```

**[observed]** (Kafka note 2, lab 1): after a group read some records, the output looked like `0  1  7  6` (committed 1, end 7, lag 6) and `1  3  3  0`. `CURRENT-OFFSET` is the **committed** offset; `CONSUMER-ID` is empty (`-`) when there is no active member, but the offsets and lag remain.

### 4.2 The admin API

What the sink does: fetch the topic's end offsets (`fetchTopicOffsets`) and the group's committed offsets (`fetchOffsets`), then compute per-partition lag with a pure function. See section 11.

### 4.3 Client-side (local) lag

The consumer already knows, for the batch it just fetched, the partition's **high watermark** and the offset of the last message: `batch.offsetLag()` (the sink uses it) **[observed in `sink-consumer.ts`]**. This costs nothing (no network call) but covers **only this member's partitions** and is **fetch-time** information.

### 4.4 Why every measurement is slightly wrong

| Source of error | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| **Committed offsets lag the real progress.** Records processed but not yet committed still count as lag | **Sawtooth**: lag rises between commits and drops at each commit. In the sink, commit happens per chunk (up to 1,000 rows), so the error is up to about one chunk plus what is fetched but unprocessed per partition |
| **End and committed offsets are read at different instants** (two requests) | Small skew; usually negligible, noticeable at very high rates |
| **The poll interval** (`LAG_POLL_MS`, 2 s) | A stale value between polls; short spikes can be missed |
| **A rebalance or coordinator trouble** | The committed offsets may be unavailable or stale; the poll fails or returns old values |
| **Gaps in offsets** (transaction markers, compaction) | `end − committed` over-counts records; irrelevant for `events.raw`, which has plain records |
| **Retention moves the log start** | A committed offset below the log start is out of range; the sink clamps lag at 0 (`Math.max(0, ...)`) but the **data is gone** (section 16) |
| **A group with no commit** | Falls back to `end − log start` (above) |

Use lag for **trends, alerting and capacity**, not as an exact count of unprocessed records.

## 5. The arithmetic

Let **λ** be the arrival rate (records/s appended to the partition or topic) and **μ** the drain rate (records/s the group finishes and commits). Then:

```
 lag(t) = lag₀ + (λ − μ) × t

 λ < μ :  lag falls;  drain time  =  lag₀ / (μ − λ)
 λ = μ :  lag constant (plateau)
 λ > μ :  lag grows linearly;  the backlog never clears until λ drops or μ rises
```

**Per partition**, the same with each partition's own λₚ and μₚ (a member processes **one partition at a time per concurrency slot**, consumer note 1, section 10).

**Worked examples** (illustrative numbers, not measurements):

| Situation | λ | μ | Starting lag | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Healthy | 2,000/s | 6,000/s | 0 | Lag stays near zero (headroom 3x) |
| Sink stopped 60 s, then restarted | 2,000/s | 6,000/s | 120,000 | Drains at 4,000/s: **30 s** |
| Same, but capacity only 2,500/s | 2,000/s | 2,500/s | 120,000 | Drains at 500/s: **240 s** |
| Database slowed to 1,500/s | 2,000/s | 1,500/s | 0 | Grows at **500/s**: after 10 min lag is 300,000 |
| Capacity equals arrival | 2,000/s | 2,000/s | 50,000 | **Never drains** |

Consequences:

- **Headroom is everything.** The time to recover from any incident is `lag / (μ − λ)`: with little spare capacity, even a short outage takes a long time to clear. The PRD's K6/K7 hypothesis states it exactly: *ingestion keeps its latency; consumer lag grows linearly with the shortfall; recovery time depends on how far the sink can exceed the arrival rate*.
- **The linear shape is diagnostic**: constant slope means a steady shortfall `λ − μ`; a change of slope means λ or μ changed.
- **Lag is bounded only by retention.** If the backlog's **age** (lag / λ at the time it was created, section 7) reaches the retention window, the oldest records are deleted unread (Kafka note 5, section 9).
- **Rates are per partition for hot keys**: a skewed key makes one partition's λₚ much larger than the average, so **that partition** can have `λₚ > μₚ` while the group as a whole looks fine (section 8).

## 6. Reading the shapes

**[reasoning]** A catalogue of lag graphs and what to suspect:

| Shape | Likely meaning | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Near zero, small flickers** | Healthy: the sawtooth of commits | Nothing |
| **Sawtooth with growing amplitude** | Batches or chunks growing; commits slowing | Commit latency, chunk time |
| **Plateau at a positive value** | Drain rate equals arrival rate: no headroom | Capacity: a small burst will not clear |
| **Steady linear growth** (all partitions) | `λ > μ`: under-provisioned, or something slowed down | Postgres latency, CPU, concurrency settings, producer rate |
| **Steady linear growth on one partition** | Hot key, or one stuck partition | Per-partition rates, key skew (producer note 6), a poison message (Step 4), a stuck owner |
| **Flat at a positive value with no throughput** | The consumer is stalled: crashed, paused, or **its commit is failing** (a last chunk stored but not committed, consumer note 3, section 9) | Members, commit errors, logs |
| **A vertical step up on all partitions** | A pause: rebalance, crash, or a stopped consumer, followed by a ramp down | Rebalance count, member count, session timeout (consumer note 2) |
| **A vertical step up on a few partitions** | The partitions of one member that died or were revoked | Which member owned them |
| **A sudden drop to ~zero** | Either a genuine catch-up, **or** the committed offset was reset to the end (`latest`) or fell out of range and was reset | Compare with throughput and **log start offset**; a drop without a matching processing rate is **data loss** (Kafka note 5, section 9) |
| **Lag oscillating with a long period** | Bursty producers and a consumer near capacity | Arrival pattern |
| **Spikes after every deploy** | Rolling restarts and rebalances | Assignor, graceful shutdown |
| **Total fine, one partition at the top** | Skew | Per-partition rates |

## 7. Lag in time

Records of lag need a rate to become time:

```
 time-lag  ≈  lag / λ          (how long the backlog took to accumulate = how old the oldest unprocessed record is)
 drain time =  lag / (μ − λ)   (how long until it is gone)
```

Why time matters more than records:

| Concern | Needs |
| --- | --- |
| **Users and the visibility target** ("events visible in Postgres within N seconds") | Time-lag / freshness |
| **Retention** (24 h window; "a consumer that falls behind retention loses data") | The **age** of the oldest unread record compared with `retention.ms` (Kafka note 5, sections 9 and 10) |
| **Capacity planning** | Drain time |
| **Alert thresholds that survive changes in traffic** | Seconds, not records |

EventPulse has a direct time metric: **`ep_event_freshness_seconds`**, a histogram of **(DB commit time − `received_at`)** per newly inserted row **[observed in `sink-stats.ts`: "End-to-end visibility: DB commit time - received_at (API), per newly inserted row"]**, with `freshness_p50_s` and `freshness_p99_s` in `/debug/stats`. Two caveats:

1. **It is measured at write time, for records just processed.** If the consumer is **completely stopped**, **no new samples arrive**, and the metric looks fine (or empty). Lag keeps growing in that case, so **you need both**: freshness describes the **past** (latency of what was just stored), lag describes the **present** (what is waiting).
2. **It only counts newly inserted rows** (not duplicates absorbed by `ON CONFLICT`), so replays after a rebalance do not distort it.

A cheap derivation of the **oldest unread age** when you need it: the age of the record at the committed offset (read its timestamp or `received_at` header) or `lag / λ` from the metrics.

## 8. Per-partition lag

The total is the sum; the **per-partition** values carry the information.

- **Hot partition.** With the measured Zipf skew (producer note 6, section 7: **34.5 %** of the traffic in the busiest of 6 partitions, **2.6 %** in the quietest) and a consumer per partition with capacity `c`, the busiest partition's λₚ is **2.07 times** the average. If `c` is just above the average rate, the busiest partition has `λₚ > μₚ` and **its lag grows linearly** while the rest sit at zero, **and the total looks like a mild problem**. The group's sustainable rate is `c / (largest share)` (producer note 6, section 7.3).
- **Stuck partition.** A poison message that makes the sink throw blocks **one** partition (Step 4's "before" note): its lag grows while the others drain.
- **Uneven recovery.** After an incident, partitions drain at different speeds; time to zero is set by the **slowest** partition.
- **Metrics design.** Label lag with **`partition`** only (low cardinality, 6 series). The sink does that; every member reports the same group-level value, so aggregate with **`max`** (or take one) in Grafana, not `sum` across members (the plan states it).
- **Which member owns it.** To act on a lagging partition you need its owner: the group describe output lists `CONSUMER-ID` per partition, and the sink logs its assignments.

## 9. What lag does not tell you

| Lag says | Lag does **not** say |
| --- | --- |
| How far behind the **commit** is | Whether the consumed records were **correct** (poison records dead-lettered also advance the offset) |
| The backlog's size | Whether rows are in Postgres: lag 0 means "committed", and equals "stored" only if the commit rule holds (consumer note 3) |
| The trend of the backlog | How old the records are (needs a rate) |
| Whether the group is keeping up | Whether data was **lost to retention** (lag may even look fine after a reset) |
| Per-partition progress | Duplicates (replays after a crash do not change lag in the end) |
| Nothing about the producers | Whether producers are failing (the API's own 503s say that) |

So lag is **necessary but not sufficient**: pair it with freshness, the duplicate counter, DLQ count, rebalance count, the member count, and the audit (Step 7).

## 10. Alerting

Principles **[reasoning]**:

1. **Alert on time, not on records.** "Oldest unprocessed record older than 60 s" survives traffic changes; "lag > 10,000" does not.
2. **Alert on trend and on stall**, not only on level: *lag increasing for 5 minutes*, and *lag > 0 with no committed-offset progress for N seconds* (a stalled or commit-failing consumer).
3. **Per partition**, with a total as a summary.
4. **Compare against retention**: warn at, say, 25 % and 50 % of `retention.ms` (Kafka note 5, section 10.1). That alert is the **only** protection against silent loss by retention.
5. **Pair with liveness**: members in the group below the expected count; a stopped consumer does not produce freshness samples.
6. **Mind the sawtooth**: evaluate over a window longer than the commit interval, or use the minimum over the window.

A starting set for the sink:

| Alert | Condition | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Backlog age | oldest unprocessed age > 60 s for 2 min | The visibility target is being missed |
| Growth | lag slope positive for 5 min | `λ > μ` |
| Stall | lag > 0 and committed offsets unchanged for 60 s | Consumer stalled, paused, or commit failing |
| Single partition | one partition's lag > 5x the median and rising | Hot key or stuck partition |
| Retention risk | oldest unprocessed age > 25 % of `retention.ms` | Time to act (Kafka note 5) |
| Members | members < expected | A sink is down |

## 11. The sink's implementation

**[observed in `lag.ts`, `sink-stats.ts`, `sink-consumer.ts`]**

**`computeLag(ends, committed)`**: a **pure function** (unit-tested) that returns, per partition, `{ partition, end, committed, lag }`:

```ts
const end = Number(e.high);  const low = Number(e.low);
const c = byPart.get(e.partition);                // committed offset, or undefined / -1 if never committed
const has = c !== undefined && c >= 0;
return { partition, end, committed: has ? c : -1, lag: Math.max(0, end - (has ? c : low)) };
```

- A **never-committed** partition lags by `end − low` (the whole log).
- `Math.max(0, ...)` keeps a committed offset beyond the end from producing negative lag.

**`LagMonitor`**: a background loop, **not** in the HTTP path:

| Aspect | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Frequency | Every `LAG_POLL_MS` (default **2,000 ms**; `0` disables) |
| Client | A **separate admin client** with client id `<clientId>-lag` and logging off |
| Calls | `admin.fetchTopicOffsets(topic)` then `admin.fetchOffsets({ groupId, topics })` |
| Result | `stats.setLag(computeLag(ends, committed))`: stores `{ at, total, byPartition }` |
| On error | Keeps the **previous** per-partition values and records the error message (truncated to 200 chars); if the client reports a disconnected state it drops the admin client and rebuilds it next time |
| Why cached | `/metrics` and `/debug/stats` **only read cached values** (no Kafka call inside an HTTP handler; the plan's rule) |
| Same on every member | The lag is **group-level**; every instance reports the same numbers |

**What it exposes**:

| Where | Name / field | Content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `/metrics` | `ep_consumer_lag{partition}` | Log end offset − committed offset of the group, per partition |
| `/metrics` | `ep_consumer_last_committed_offset{partition}` | Committed offset per partition (`-1` = never committed) |
| `/metrics` | `ep_event_freshness_seconds` | Histogram: DB commit − `received_at`, per newly inserted row |
| `/debug/stats` | `lag.total`, `lag.by_partition`, `lag.last_committed`, `lag.polled_at`, `lag.error` | The cached group lag |
| `/debug/stats` | `local_lag` | Per partition, from the **last batch this member read** (`batch.offsetLag()`); cheap, this member only |
| `/debug/stats` | `freshness_p50_s`, `freshness_p99_s` | Quantiles of the freshness histogram |

**Local lag versus group lag**: local lag is available instantly with every batch but covers only the partitions this member owns and was **sampled when the batch was fetched**; the group lag is fresher in time and covers everything but costs a poll. Use group lag for dashboards and alerts, local lag for a quick "am I draining" check inside the process.

## 12. Lag in the audit and the experiments

**The audit** (Step 7): before it takes its **end snapshot**, `audit.sh end` **waits until consumer lag is 0** (or `AUDIT_DRAIN_TIMEOUT_S`, 120 s by default); if it times out the audit reports **"not drained"** (exit code 2) rather than a false mismatch. Check **I** is "consumer lag at end == 0". So lag drives when a run is considered finished, and the audit must read the **same** committed offsets the metrics do.

**The experiments**:

| Experiment | What lag shows |
| --- | --- |
| K3 partitions versus consumers | Per-partition lag and which members own what; scaling stops at the partition count |
| K6/K7 slow database | Linear lag growth while ingestion latency stays flat; recovery time `lag / (μ − λ)` |
| K8 rebalances | Vertical steps in lag during rebalances, and lag ramps while a crashed member's partitions wait for the session timeout |
| K9 hot key | One partition's lag growing; the ceiling `c / largest share` |
| K10 unbounded queue | The API side; lag is the Kafka-side counterpart |
| K11 `earliest` versus `latest` | Lag after a reset: the `latest` run shows lag dropping to ~0 while data is skipped |
| Retention experiments | Lag versus the log start offset |

## 16. Edge cases

| Case | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Consumer stopped | Lag grows at `λ`; freshness stops updating; members drop |
| A commit fails on the last chunk of a partition | That partition's lag stays above 0 (consumer note 3) |
| Never-committed partition | Lag = end − log start |
| Committed offset beyond the end (after an unclean election) | Negative raw lag, clamped to 0 by the sink; the reset policy applies |
| Committed offset below the log start (retention) | Lag is computed from the committed offset, so it can be **larger than what still exists**; the data between the log start and the committed offset is gone; after the reset, lag **drops** |
| Group deleted or offsets expired | Looks like a never-committed group |
| Admin poll fails | The previous lag values stay with an `error` string; do not trust "stable" lag for long without a fresh `polled_at` |
| Rebalance in progress | The committed offsets may briefly be stale; lag shows a step |
| Several groups on the same topic | Each has its own lag |
| Topic with compaction or transactions | Offset gaps make lag over-count records |
| Very high rates | The sawtooth amplitude (a chunk or two per partition) matters less |

## 18. Common confusions

1. **"Lag is the number of unprocessed records."** It is `end − committed`: an upper bound that includes processed-but-uncommitted records.
2. **"Zero lag means everything is stored."** It means everything is committed.
3. **"A big lag is bad, a small lag is good."** What matters is **age** and **trend**; 50,000 records can be a second or an hour.
4. **"Total lag is enough."** Per-partition lag finds hot and stuck partitions.
5. **"Lag only grows when consumers are slow."** Also when producers speed up, or when a partition is stuck.
6. **"If lag suddenly drops to zero, the consumer caught up."** It may have been reset (`latest` or out of range): data may have been skipped.
7. **"Lag is a property of the topic."** It is per group.
8. **"Freshness replaces lag."** Freshness describes processed records; a stopped consumer produces none.
9. **"More consumers always reduce lag."** Only up to the partition count and the skew limit.
10. **"Lag is measured in real time."** From polled committed offsets, with a sawtooth and a polling delay.
11. **"Local lag and group lag are the same."** Local is this member's partitions at fetch time; group is all partitions at poll time.
12. **"A lag alert should use a fixed number of records."** Use time, trend and stall conditions.

## 20. Cheat sheet

### Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Lag | Log end offset − committed offset, per partition |
| Total lag | Sum over partitions |
| Time-lag | Age of the oldest unprocessed record |
| Local lag | High watermark − last offset of the batch just fetched |
| Drain rate (μ) | Records per second the group finishes and commits |
| Arrival rate (λ) | Records per second appended |
| Freshness | DB commit time − `received_at` (the sink's end-to-end visibility) |
| Sawtooth | Lag's rise-and-drop pattern from commit granularity |
| Headroom | μ − λ |

### Formulas

```
lag(p)        = end(p) − committed(p)
lag(t)        = lag₀ + (λ − μ) t
drain time    = lag₀ / (μ − λ)              (only if μ > λ)
time-lag      ≈ lag / λ
```

### The sink's lag (observed)

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Poll interval | `LAG_POLL_MS` = 2,000 ms, separate admin client |
| Metrics | `ep_consumer_lag{partition}`, `ep_consumer_last_committed_offset{partition}`, `ep_event_freshness_seconds` |
| `/debug/stats` | `lag.{total,by_partition,last_committed,polled_at,error}`, `local_lag`, `freshness_p50_s`, `freshness_p99_s` |
| Never-committed partition | lag = end − log start |
