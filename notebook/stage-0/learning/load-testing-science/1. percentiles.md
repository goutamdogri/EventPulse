# Percentiles: p50, p90, p99, and p999

In backend engineering, percentiles measure how long requests take for different portions of your users. They help you understand not just typical performance, but also the slow requests that averages can hide.

For EventPulse, measuring latency percentiles is essential because a small fraction of slow ingestion requests can cause timeouts, retries, and cascading failures.

## 1. What does a percentile mean?

Suppose you collect the response times of 1,000 requests and sort them from fastest to slowest.

| Metric       | Meaning                                            | Example              |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------- |
| p50          | 50% of requests completed at or below this latency | 5 ms                 |
| p90          | 90% completed at or below this latency             | 12 ms                |
| p99          | 99% completed at or below this latency             | 80 ms                |
| p999 (p99.9) | 99.9% completed at or below this latency           | 2,000 ms (2 seconds) |

The numbers above are illustrative.

Think of the percentile as a boundary in the sorted list. At p99, roughly 990 out of 1,000 requests are at or below the measured latency, while roughly 10 are slower.

At p99.9, approximately 999 out of 1,000 requests are at or below the boundary, leaving roughly one request slower.

Important: A percentile is a threshold, not the latency of every request in that group. Requests above p99 can take very different amounts of time.

## 2. Why averages hide pain

Consider two services handling 1,000 requests each.

Service A: consistent latency

```txt
Most requests:  5 ms
Mean:           5 ms
p99:            5 ms
```

Service B: a long tail

```txt
999 requests:   approximately 3 ms each
1 request:      approximately 2,000 ms
Mean:           approximately 5 ms
p999:           approximately 2,000 ms
```

Service B has nearly the same mean latency, but one request is extremely slow.

This illustrates the key point: the mean describes the overall arithmetic average; it doesn't tell you how latency is distributed.

The exact percentiles depend on the complete set of observations. The example is illustrative rather than a precise percentile calculation for every boundary.

## 3. How to calculate the mean versus a percentile

Suppose five requests take:

```txt
2 ms, 3 ms, 4 ms, 5 ms, 100 ms
```

The mean is:

$$ \frac{2+3+4+5+100}{5}=22.8\text{ ms} $$

The median (p50) is 4 ms.

Sorted values:

```txt
  2    3    4    5                                  100
  |----|----|----|------------------------------------|
                    One unusually slow request
```

The mean is pulled upward by the 100 ms request, while the median remains representative of the middle observation. With larger datasets, high percentiles help expose the slow tail.

## 4. Why p99 and p999 matter at scale

Suppose EventPulse processes 100,000 requests per second.

- At p99, approximately 1% of requests are slower than the p99 threshold.
- At p99.9, approximately 0.1% are slower than the p99.9 threshold.

That corresponds to approximately:

- 1,000 requests per second beyond the p99 threshold.
- 100 requests per second beyond the p99.9 threshold.

These are approximate rates under a sustained workload, not guarantees about individual time windows.

A tiny percentage becomes a substantial number of slow requests at high traffic.

Slow requests can also hold resources longer, trigger client retries, and increase load on downstream systems. This can create a feedback loop:

```txt
Slow database or Kafka
          |
          v
Requests take longer
          |
          v
More in-flight requests
          |
          v
More resource pressure
          |
          v
Even higher tail latency
```

This is why a service with an acceptable mean can still be unhealthy.

## 5. Measure percentiles in EventPulse

Track at least these metrics for the ingestion API:

- Request latency: p50, p90, p99, p99.9 where measurement quality permits.
- Request throughput: requests/events per second.
- Error and timeout rates.
- Queue or Kafka publishing latency.
- Database latency, if synchronous database work is involved.
- CPU utilization and event-loop lag.

For Prometheus, a histogram can record request durations:

```js
import { Histogram } from 'prom-client';

const requestDuration = new Histogram({
  name: 'eventpulse_ingestion_duration_seconds',
  help: 'Event ingestion request duration',
  labelNames: ['method', 'status'],
  buckets: [
    0.001, 0.0025, 0.005, 0.01,
    0.025, 0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2,
  ],
});
```

You can estimate p99 over a five-minute window in Prometheus:

```txt
histogram_quantile(
  0.99,
  sum by (le) (
    rate(eventpulse_ingestion_duration_seconds_bucket[5m])
  )
)
```

This returns an estimated p99 latency in seconds. Histogram bucket boundaries affect the accuracy of the estimate, so choose buckets that cover the latency range you care about. Add sufficiently fine buckets around your service's latency objectives.

## 6. One subtle but important issue: percentiles don't combine simply

If one API instance has p99 = 10 ms and another has p99 = 100 ms, averaging those two values does not give the overall p99.

To estimate a fleet-wide percentile, aggregate the underlying histogram bucket counts first, then calculate the quantile. Also consider traffic volume, time windows, and whether you need per-endpoint or per-customer breakdowns.

Remember: p50 tells you what a typical request experiences; p99 and p999 reveal the slow tail. Use percentiles alongside the mean, throughput, and error rate to determine whether EventPulse is actually meeting its latency objectives.
