## 1. TL;DR

- A sender that did not receive an acknowledgment is in a state of **uncertainty**: the action may have **failed**, or **succeeded with the reply lost**. From the sender's side these two outcomes produce **identical observations** (silence).
- The sender has only two moves: **retry** (risk a duplicate if it had succeeded) or **give up** (risk loss if it had failed). **There is no third move that is safe in both worlds**, unless the receiver can recognize a repeat.
- The **Two Generals problem** is the formal version: two parties communicating over a lossy channel **cannot reach certain common knowledge** that they both agreed to act, no matter how many messages they exchange. Adding more acknowledgments only moves the uncertainty to the last message.
- Therefore **duplicates (or loss) are inevitable at every hop** where the sender cannot see the receiver's state. Choosing at-least-once picks "duplicate" over "loss".
- The solution is not to eliminate the ambiguity (impossible) but to make it **harmless**: give every action a **unique id** and make the receiver **idempotent** so a repeat has no extra effect.
- Duplicates in EventPulse come from at least **eight** distinct places (section 10), at different layers. No single mechanism covers all of them; defense in depth is the design.

> **One-line version:** _You cannot know if the other side did it, so you must be willing to ask twice; the only thing you control is whether asking twice is harmless._

## 2. The mental model

You phone a restaurant to book a table. The line goes dead right after you say "Friday at 8, name Dogri".

| You do                   | Outcome A (they heard)                         | Outcome B (they did not) |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Do nothing               | Table booked. Good.                            | **No table. Lost.**      |
| Call back and book again | **Two bookings** under your name (a duplicate) | Table booked. Good.      |

You cannot tell A from B. Both look like a dead line. Your options:

1. **Do not call back** (at-most-once): you risk arriving to no table.
2. **Call back** (at-least-once): you risk two tables blocked.
3. **Call back and say "this is a repeat of my earlier call: same name, same time"** (idempotent request): if they have the first booking they say "already have it"; if they do not, they book it. **Both outcomes end in exactly one table.** That works because the restaurant can recognize your repeat. The recognition key is _name + date + time_, your idempotency key.

Everything in distributed delivery is that phone call.

## 3. The ambiguity: what a timeout really tells you

A **timeout** is a statement about **time**, not about **fate**. It means: "I waited T and did not hear back."

### 3.1 Four worlds behind one observation

```
Sender (S) ---- request ----> Receiver (R) ---- response ----> S

World 1: request lost in transit             S sends, R never sees it           effect: none
World 2: R crashed before processing         R sees it, dies before acting      effect: none
World 3: R processed, crashed before reply   R acts, dies before replying       effect: DONE
World 4: R processed, reply lost in transit  R acts, replies, reply dropped     effect: DONE
World 5: R is just slow                      R will act (or has acted) later    effect: DONE, soon
```

Observation by S in **all five** worlds: _no response within T_.

Because worlds 3, 4 and 5 have **already produced the effect**, a retry from S produces the effect **a second time** unless R can detect the repeat. Because worlds 1 and 2 have **not**, giving up means the effect **never** happens.

### 3.2 Why "just check first" does not work

Natural reply: "Before retrying, ask R whether it processed my request."

- That query is **another message** over the same unreliable channel. It can also time out. The uncertainty simply recurs one level up.
- Even when the query succeeds, **the answer can be stale** by the time you act on it (R may process the original in the meantime: a **race** between the delayed original and your retry).
- The check-then-act sequence is itself a **non-atomic read-then-write** on R's side, creating a new race.

This is why the robust answer is to push the check into **R's atomic operation** itself: a unique constraint, `SET NX`, a conditional write. The check and the write are one step, so there is no race.

## 4. The Two Generals problem

### 4.1 The setup

Two armies, A and B, sit on two hills on either side of an enemy city. They win only if they **attack at the same time**. They communicate only by messengers who must pass through enemy territory and may be **captured** (messages can be lost).

General A decides "attack at dawn" and sends a messenger. Does A attack?

### 4.2 The argument

1. A sends "attack at dawn". A does not know it arrived, so A **cannot safely attack alone**.
2. B receives it. B knows A wants to attack but **B does not know A knows B got it**. So B replies "agreed". But if B attacks and A never gets the reply, A stays home and B is **alone**.
3. A receives the "agreed". Now A knows B knows. But **B does not know that A got the reply**, so B needs a confirmation of the confirmation.
4. Each new acknowledgment leaves the **sender of the last message** uncertain about whether it arrived.

This recursion has no end. For **any finite protocol** of N messages, consider the **last** one. It might be lost. If the protocol is correct, the outcome must not depend on that last message arriving (otherwise the sender of it is in doubt). But then that message was unnecessary: remove it. Repeat the argument on the new last message. By induction, **no message is necessary**, which is absurd because with zero messages nobody coordinated anything.

### 4.3 Formal conclusion

> **Over a channel that can lose messages, no finite exchange of messages can give both parties certainty (common knowledge) that both will act.**

The same statement, in database words: **you cannot achieve atomic commit between two parties over an unreliable channel without an assumption that cannot be implemented in general** (reliable delivery in bounded time, or a trusted third party that can itself fail). Two-phase commit does not escape it; it merely moves the uncertainty to the coordinator ("blocking" when the coordinator dies after prepare).

### 4.4 Translating generals into a producer and a broker

| Generals                                     | Producer/broker                                                                         |
| -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Attack at dawn"                             | "Append this record to the log"                                                         |
| Messenger captured                           | Packet dropped, connection reset, response timeout                                      |
| A's uncertainty whether B got it             | Producer's uncertainty whether the broker appended                                      |
| "Both attack or neither"                     | "Exactly one copy in the log"                                                           |
| The guarantee impossible with messages alone | Exactly-once **delivery** impossible; exactly-once **effect** needs receiver-side state |

### 4.5 Why the practical systems are not paralyzed by it

The problem says you cannot get **certainty over a lossy channel with no other assumptions**. Real systems **change the question**:

- Instead of "agree to act together with certainty", ask "**make acting twice harmless**". Then A can just attack repeatedly; B's repeated attacks add no extra damage.
- Or "**make repeats detectable**". A message with an id lets B say "already did that".

The problem is a theorem about _certainty_; engineering works with _tolerance_.

## 5. Why acknowledgments of acknowledgments do not help

A common instinct is to add layers: ack, ack-of-ack, and so on. Show why that fails to restore certainty, with a TCP-flavored version.

```
S ──req──> R
S <──ack── R           (S now knows R got the request, assuming this ack is not lost)
S ──ack2─> R           (R now knows S got the ack)
...
```

- After the final message, **its sender cannot know if it arrived**. The uncertainty is always on the last hop. Adding another message shifts the problem to the new last hop.
- TCP does give **reliable in-order byte delivery between two endpoints while the connection lives**, by retransmitting segments until acked. But TCP is **at-least-once at the segment level made invisible by sequence numbers** (the receiver discards duplicate segments). It still **cannot tell the application whether the peer processed the last bytes before the connection broke**. If a connection resets, the application is back in the world-3/4 ambiguity: it does not know whether the peer read and acted on the final request.

**Application-level consequence:** "the HTTP request went over TCP" does not mean "the server handled it exactly once". After a reset the client must decide retry-or-not, and the server must deal with repeats.

### 5.1 Retransmission with sequence numbers is the template for idempotence

TCP solves _its_ duplicate problem exactly the way Stage 3 does: **number the things; the receiver remembers the highest number it has seen and drops repeats**. Kafka's idempotent producer is the same trick at a higher layer (note 4). The **application-level idempotency key** is the same trick one layer higher again. One idea, three layers.

## 6. What the impossibility does and does not say

### 6.1 It does say

- No protocol of messages alone can guarantee **both** no-loss **and** no-duplicate **delivery** under message loss.
- Any **timeout-based** decision can be wrong.
- Any "exactly-once" claim must name **extra assumptions or state** (unique ids, a transaction store, a single writer, bounded time).

### 6.2 It does not say

- It does **not** say distributed systems cannot be correct. They are, by tolerating duplicates.
- It does **not** say retries are bad. Retries are the **only** way to avoid loss; the problem is retries **without** recognition.
- It does **not** say consensus is impossible. Consensus algorithms (Raft, Paxos, the protocol behind Kafka's controller) work by assuming a **majority** of nodes remain reachable and by using timeouts as failure _detectors_ (imperfect but good enough), accepting that they may block when the assumption fails.

### 6.3 FLP, in a sentence

The **FLP result** (Fischer, Lynch, Paterson) says that in a fully **asynchronous** system (no bound on message delay), **no deterministic consensus protocol can be guaranteed to terminate if even one node may crash**, because a slow node is indistinguishable from a crashed one. This is the _same_ ambiguity as world 5 in section 3: **slow vs dead**. Practical systems escape FLP using timeouts (assuming "partial synchrony") and randomization, trading guaranteed termination for **probabilistic** progress. You do not need the proof; you need the intuition: **you cannot tell slow from dead**, hence timeouts, hence the possibility of acting on a wrong guess, hence duplicates and split-brain.

## 7. The two escape routes: tolerate and recognize

Since certainty is impossible, there are exactly two engineering strategies.

| Strategy      | Idea                                                                | Examples                                                                              |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Tolerate**  | Make the operation **idempotent** so a repeat is a no-op            | `SET`, upsert, `ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING`, `PUT` semantic                               |
| **Recognize** | Attach an **id** and keep a **record of seen ids**; discard repeats | TCP sequence numbers, Kafka producer sequence numbers, idempotency keys, inbox tables |

They are two views of one thing: an idempotent receiver is a receiver whose state **already encodes** "have I seen this?". The unique constraint on `event_id` is both: the table is the effect **and** the record of seen ids.

A third, partial route, **fencing**, is used when the danger is not repeat messages but **two actors who both believe they are in charge** (see 8.4 and note 3 on zombie producers).

## 8. Failure models: crash, omission, partition, slow

Different faults create ambiguity differently. A careful design lists which it handles.

### 8.1 Crash-stop and crash-recovery

A process dies. With **crash-recovery**, it later restarts with only its **durable** state. Every in-memory fact ("I already sent message 17") is gone. This is the commonest source of duplicates in practice: the restarted consumer has only the **committed** offset, which lags what it actually did (section 10, source E).

### 8.2 Omission (message loss)

A network drops a packet or a response. It is the Two Generals setting.

### 8.3 Delay and reorder

A message arrives **late** or **out of order**. A delayed original can arrive **after** the retry: now two copies exist even though each send looked "failed". Also relevant to **stale writes**: an old update overwriting a newer one (relevant to the idempotent-by-upsert design: pair upserts with a version or timestamp guard when updates, not inserts, are involved).

### 8.4 Pause (stop-the-world)

A process freezes for seconds (GC pause, VM stall, `SIGSTOP`, laptop sleep), then resumes **believing nothing changed**. Meanwhile the cluster declared it dead and gave its work to another process. Now **two** workers think they own the partition: a **zombie**. Without a fence (an epoch or generation number that rejects the old one), both write. This is the mechanism behind consumer-group rebalances causing overlapping processing, and behind Kafka's **producer epoch** for transactions (note 3).

### 8.5 Partition (network split)

A group of nodes cannot talk to another group. Each side may continue independently. On healing, work done on both sides may overlap. Kafka mitigates with ISR, leader epochs and `min.insync.replicas`; applications mitigate with idempotent effects.

### 8.6 The summary

| Fault     | Creates ambiguity about | Typical duplicate or loss                    |
| --------- | ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Crash     | "How far did I get?"    | Replay from the last durable progress marker |
| Omission  | "Did it arrive?"        | Resend                                       |
| Delay     | "Is it lost or late?"   | Resend then late original arrives            |
| Pause     | "Am I still the owner?" | Two writers                                  |
| Partition | "Who is still alive?"   | Divergent work on both sides                 |

## 9. Timeouts are guesses, not facts

Every retry decision is triggered by a timeout, and a timeout is a **threshold on elapsed time**, chosen by a human.

### 9.1 The tradeoff

| Timeout too short                                                                                                                                | Timeout too long                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Healthy-but-slow requests are declared failed, so **retried while the first is still in flight**: **more duplicates**, extra load, a retry storm | Real failures are detected slowly: high tail latency, stuck threads, slow failover |

There is **no correct value**, only a tradeoff against the latency distribution. A timeout set below the 99th percentile latency **manufactures duplicates for healthy traffic**.

### 9.2 Timeouts in the Kafka producer (so you know which one fired)

| Setting                       | Meaning (typical default)                           | Ambiguity if it fires                                                             |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `request.timeout.ms` (30 s)   | Wait for a broker response to one request           | The batch may have been **appended** on the leader                                |
| `delivery.timeout.ms` (120 s) | Upper bound on the whole `send()` including retries | The record may be in the log **even though** `send()` reported failure to the app |
| `max.block.ms`                | Wait for metadata or buffer space                   | Record was **not** sent; safe to retry                                            |
| `transaction.timeout.ms`      | Transaction coordinator aborts an open transaction  | Transaction was **aborted**; writes are invisible to `read_committed`             |

Key subtlety: a producer callback that says **"failed: delivery timeout"** does **not** mean "not written". It means "I stopped trying". An application that retries on that callback can create a duplicate that the idempotent producer **cannot** catch because it is a _new_ `send()` (note 4).

### 9.3 Failure detectors

Group membership is also timeout-based: a consumer that does not heartbeat within `session.timeout.ms`, or does not call `poll()` within `max.poll.interval.ms`, is **declared dead** and its partitions reassigned, even if it is alive and mid-batch. That is the "pause then zombie" scenario of 8.4 in daily clothes. See Stage 2 consumer notes on rebalances.

## 10. Where duplicates are born in EventPulse: the full catalogue

Walk the pipeline from the client to Postgres and list every place the ambiguity appears. Each is a separate source with a separate cure.

```
client ─HTTP─> ingest-api ─produce─> Kafka ─consume─> consumer-sink ─SQL─> Postgres
```

| #   | Source                                                                                     | Layer          | The ambiguity                                      | Duplicate created                                                        | Cured by                                                                                      |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A   | Client retries after a timeout or 5xx                                                      | Client to API  | Did the API accept it?                             | Two POSTs with the same payload become two events                        | API idempotency key (`event_id` or `Idempotency-Key`)                                         |
| B   | User or SDK sends the same event twice deliberately or by bug                              | Client         | Not an ambiguity; a genuine second request         | Same event twice                                                         | Same idempotency key; **different payload under the same key must be reported as a conflict** |
| C   | Library retries a produce whose **response was lost**                                      | API to broker  | Did the broker append?                             | Same batch appended twice                                                | **Idempotent producer** (PID + sequence)                                                      |
| D   | Application code retries `send()` after a reported failure                                 | API to broker  | Failure reported, but the record may be in the log | A new send with a **new sequence**                                       | Key-level dedup downstream; idempotent sink                                                   |
| E   | Consumer commits **offset after** the DB commit, then dies in the gap                      | Broker to sink | How far did I really get?                          | Rows reprocessed after restart                                           | **Idempotent sink** (`ON CONFLICT`)                                                           |
| F   | Rebalance hands the partition to another consumer while the old one is mid-batch or paused | Consumer group | Who owns the partition?                            | Same range processed by two members, or reprocessed from the last commit | Idempotent sink; offset commits fenced by generation                                          |
| G   | Offset commit **request fails** (broker unavailable) after the DB commit                   | Broker to sink | Did the commit land?                               | Reprocess on restart                                                     | Idempotent sink                                                                               |
| H   | Operator replays a topic range, a retry-tier message or a DLQ entry                        | Operations     | Intentional repeat                                 | The same event enters again                                              | Idempotent sink; replay tool dry-run; `event_id` preserved on replay                          |
| I   | Retry topic re-publishes a message **and** the original is also reprocessed                | Pipeline       | Was the original handled?                          | Duplicate in main and retry paths                                        | Idempotent sink; retry carries original `event_id`                                            |
| J   | Producer **restart** resends buffered or unacked records under a new PID                   | API            | What was acked before the crash?                   | New sequences, so the broker sees "new" records                          | Idempotent sink                                                                               |
| K   | Two `ingest-api` instances both process a retried request routed by a load balancer        | API            | Which instance got it first?                       | Two producers, two PIDs                                                  | Shared dedup (Redis) at API or sink constraint                                                |
| L   | Mirror or replication pipeline (MirrorMaker, CDC) resends after reconnect                  | Infra          | Where did the mirror stop?                         | Repeats across clusters                                                  | Idempotent destination                                                                        |

### 10.1 Observations from the catalogue

1. **Eight or more independent sources.** A and B at the edge, C and D at the producer, E-G in the consumer, H-L in operations and infrastructure.
2. **Only C is removed by Kafka's idempotent producer.** All the others survive it. This is the central claim of note 4.
3. **E, F, G, H, I, J, K, L all end at the sink.** One idempotent sink absorbs all of them. That is why the sink constraint is the **last line of defense** and the **non-negotiable** control.
4. **A, B, K are cheaper to stop early** (at the API with Redis) to save Kafka and Postgres work, but an early-layer miss must never cause incorrectness, because the sink still protects storage.

### 10.2 Defense in depth, as a table

| Layer    | Control                                             | What it protects                 | Failure window               |
| -------- | --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| API      | Idempotency key (Redis `SET NX EX`)                 | Fast rejection of client retries | Redis TTL, Redis loss        |
| Producer | `enable.idempotence`                                | Library-retry duplicates         | One producer session         |
| Consumer | Commit after DB commit                              | Prevents **loss**                | Creates the duplicate window |
| Sink     | `PRIMARY KEY (event_id)` + `ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING` | Everything that reaches storage  | **None** for stored events   |

The weakest-but-fastest layers are **optimizations**; the strongest-but-latest layer is the **guarantee**.

## 11. Duplicates you cannot prevent, only absorb

Some duplicate sources are **intrinsic** to the design and cannot be removed without giving up something worse. Be explicit:

| Duplicate                                              | Why it cannot be prevented                                        | Why prevention would be worse                       |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Reprocessing after a crash in the effect-to-offset gap | The two writes are in different systems                           | Committing the offset first would **lose** messages |
| Rebalance overlap                                      | Failure detection is timeout-based and cannot tell slow from dead | Longer timeouts slow every failover                 |
| Client retry after a timeout                           | The client cannot know the outcome                                | Never retrying means loss on every transient fault  |
| Replay for recovery                                    | Replay is a deliberate feature                                    | Not replaying makes disaster recovery impossible    |

You manage these by making them **cheap, counted and harmless**, not by trying to reach zero. A "zero duplicates" requirement is a design smell; the correct requirement is "**zero duplicate effects**".

## 12. Loss is also inevitable if you refuse duplicates

The symmetric truth, often forgotten: a system that prevents duplicates **by never retrying** accepts **loss**, and loss is not observable.

| Choice                      | Under a timeout           | Under 1,000 timeouts/day  | Detectable?                       |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Never retry                 | Message may be lost       | Up to 1,000 silent losses | No (nothing logs a missing event) |
| Retry blindly               | Message may be duplicated | Up to 1,000 duplicates    | Yes (if you count)                |
| Retry + idempotent receiver | Neither                   | Neither                   | Yes (counters)                    |

The asymmetry is why mature systems choose "retry + dedup": **a visible, bounded, fixable problem beats an invisible one.**

## 13. Retries done correctly: backoff, jitter, budgets

If retries are unavoidable, make them safe for the **system**, not just for the one message.

### 13.1 Rules

1. **Retry only idempotent or keyed operations.** Retrying a non-idempotent call without a key is manufacturing duplicates.
2. **Retry only transient errors.** Network timeout, 503, leader not available: yes. Validation error, 400, schema mismatch: no (permanent failures belong in a DLQ).
3. **Exponential backoff with jitter.** Wait `base * 2^attempt` plus a random fraction, so many clients do not retry in lockstep (a **retry storm** / thundering herd that turns a blip into an outage).
4. **Bounded attempts and a deadline.** Unlimited retries convert a downstream outage into upstream overload and unbounded latency.
5. **A retry budget.** Cap retries to a fraction of normal traffic (for example 10%) so that when the dependency is down, retries do not multiply load.
6. **Propagate the same idempotency key on every retry.** A new key on each attempt defeats the whole mechanism.

### 13.2 Amplification

If each of three layers retries three times, one failed call becomes **27** downstream calls. Retries compound across layers. Retry at **one** well-chosen layer and let the others fail fast, or use budgets.

### 13.3 The timeout hierarchy

Inner timeouts must be **shorter** than outer ones, otherwise an outer layer retries while the inner layer is still working, producing concurrent duplicates:

```
client timeout (10 s) > API handler timeout (8 s) > produce delivery.timeout (5 s) > broker request timeout (2 s)
```

If inverted, the client gives up and retries while the API is still sending the first attempt: **guaranteed duplicates** under load.

## 14. Idempotency as the general answer

The principle (Pat Helland's "Idempotence Is Not a Medical Condition"): in a world of at-least-once messaging, **every receiver must be designed so that processing the same message again is not harmful**.

Three levels of design:

| Level                                                          | Technique                                   | Example                             |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| **Operation is idempotent by nature**                          | Set, upsert, delete-by-id                   | `INSERT ... ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING` |
| **Operation made idempotent by an id**                         | Dedup table, inbox, `SET NX`                | `idem:{tenant}:{key}`               |
| **State machine ignores out-of-order or repeated transitions** | Version numbers, "only move forward" guards | `UPDATE ... WHERE version < $new`   |

Key design points (expanded in the idempotency note):

- **Who generates the id:** the **origin** (client or first producer), once, **before** any retry, so every retry carries the same one.
- **Scope:** per tenant, so tenants cannot collide.
- **Lifetime:** as long as a duplicate can plausibly arrive. For the sink's unique constraint, effectively forever.
- **Same id, different payload:** a bug; surface it as a conflict instead of silently accepting either.

## 15. Reasoning drills: finding the ambiguity in any design

A 4-step method you can apply to **any** diagram or interview question.

1. **Draw each hop** as `sender -> receiver`.
2. For each hop ask: "**If the sender hears nothing, what are the possible states?**" (always: not done, done).
3. Ask: "**What does the sender do?**" (retry or give up). That determines **duplicate or loss**.
4. Ask: "**What on the receiver makes a repeat harmless?**" If nothing, you found a bug.

### 15.1 Worked drill A: the outbox relay

A relay reads outbox rows, publishes to Kafka, then marks them published.

- Hop: relay -> Kafka. Relay dies after publish, before marking.
- Ambiguity: was the row published? State is **done**, relay believes **not done**.
- Move: on restart it republishes. Duplicate in Kafka.
- Cure: consumers idempotent, keyed by the outbox row id. (Confirms why the outbox is "at-least-once".)

### 15.2 Worked drill B: "charge the customer then send the receipt"

- Charge succeeds; process dies before the receipt is sent.
- On restart, a naive handler charges again.
- Cure: the charge call carries an idempotency key derived from the order id; the processor returns the **original** result on repeat.

### 15.3 Worked drill C: a consumer that reads "now" in its handler

If the stored row's value depends on `now()` and processing is repeated, the second run writes a **different** value. The sink's `DO NOTHING` keeps the first, which is fine; but with `DO UPDATE` the value would drift. Lesson: stamp times **once**, at the origin (`received_at` in the API), and carry them in the message.

## 16. EventPulse decisions

| Decision                                    | Choice                                                                                               | Reason                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Accept that duplicates cannot be eliminated | Yes; document each source (catalogue in section 10)                                                  | Fighting the impossible wastes effort and creates loss      |
| Primary strategy                            | Make duplicates harmless: idempotent sink                                                            | Works for every source from E through L                     |
| Secondary strategies                        | `enable.idempotence=true`; API-level idempotency key                                                 | Cut duplicate volume and give early, clear errors           |
| Id origin                                   | `event_id` generated by the client or the first producer, before any retry                           | Stable across retries                                       |
| Timestamp origin                            | `received_at` set once at ingest and carried in the message                                          | Reprocessing must not change stored values                  |
| Retry policy (later in Stage 3)             | Backoff + jitter + bounded attempts, then DLQ                                                        | Avoid retry storms and poison loops                         |
| Failure ledger                              | Each duplicate source from section 10 gets a row: how triggered, how counted, which layer absorbs it | Required to write the delivery guarantees document honestly |

## 17. Common confusions

1. **"Increase the timeout and duplicates disappear."** They shrink but never vanish; a crash after acting still hides the result.
2. **"Use TCP, so no duplicates."** TCP de-duplicates its own segments inside one connection. It cannot tell your application whether the peer finished processing before a reset.
3. **"Two-phase commit solves Two Generals."** It reduces the window but blocks when the coordinator fails at the wrong moment. It is a different tradeoff, not an escape.
4. **"The retry will fail if the first succeeded."** Only if the receiver is built to detect it. Otherwise it succeeds again.
5. **"If `send()` reported an error, the message is not in Kafka."** Not necessarily (delivery timeout after the broker appended).
6. **"Duplicates mean a bug."** A duplicate _attempt_ is expected behavior of a correct at-least-once system. A duplicate _effect_ is the bug.
7. **"Idempotent consumer makes retries free."** It makes them _safe_. They still cost CPU, I/O and latency.
8. **"Network is reliable inside one datacenter."** Pauses, GC, noisy neighbors, bufferbloat and restarts happen anyway. The Two Generals problem is about _fault_, not _distance_.

## 19. Cheat sheet

### Glossary

| Term                | Meaning                                                                                                        |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ambiguity           | Sender cannot tell "not done" from "done, reply lost"                                                          |
| Two Generals        | Impossibility of certain agreement over a lossy channel                                                        |
| FLP                 | Impossibility of guaranteed consensus in a pure async system with a crash; slow is indistinguishable from dead |
| Timeout             | A time threshold used to guess failure                                                                         |
| Retry storm         | Many clients retrying in sync, amplifying an outage                                                            |
| Jitter              | Randomness added to backoff delays                                                                             |
| Zombie              | A process that was declared dead but resumes and keeps acting                                                  |
| Fencing             | Rejecting a stale actor using an epoch or generation number                                                    |
| Inbox / dedup store | Record of processed ids on the receiver                                                                        |
| Effect              | The observable state change, as opposed to the message                                                         |

### Rules

1. A timeout is a guess. Design for the guess being wrong in both directions.
2. Retry plus idempotence beats giving up.
3. The id must originate **before** the first attempt and stay constant across retries.
4. Dedup at the **last** step is the guarantee; dedup earlier is an optimization.
5. Retry at one layer, with backoff, jitter, a bounded count and a budget.
6. Inner timeouts shorter than outer.
7. Aim for zero duplicate **effects**, not zero duplicate **messages**.

### The impossibility in one picture

```
 S ── msg ──> R
 S <── ack ── R         S knows R got msg  ... if this ack arrived
 S ── ack2 ─> R         R knows S got ack  ... if this arrived
 ...                    the last message is always unconfirmed -> always one side in doubt
```
